Leader Election for auto-recovery

Something has to restart a server when it dies. But what restarts that something? These notes cover how the lesson ends this endless chain, then go deeper on the parts an interviewer will push on.

7 min 45 s video Blue sections · from the video Amber sections · extra depth

The whole lesson in five lines

  1. When a server dies, a program called an orchestrator starts a new one, so no human has to.
  2. But the orchestrator can die too. If you add a second orchestrator to watch the first, you then need a third to watch the second. The chain never ends.
  3. The fix: run several copies of the orchestrator. One is the leader, the rest are workers. Workers watch the servers. The leader watches the workers.
  4. If the leader dies, the workers notice and hold a leader election: they pick one of themselves as the new leader. Nobody outside the group is needed.
  5. That is why the chain stops here. The idea is general — use it in any system that must recover by itself.

1

Who restarts a dead server?

From the video▶ 0:15

Say you have a few servers answering HTTP requests behind a load balancer (a box that spreads requests across them). One of them crashes. You need a new one to take its place.

You do not want a person to do this at 3 am. So you add another program whose only job is to do it for you. The lesson calls it an orchestrator:

  • It keeps an eye on all the servers.
  • When one goes down, it starts a new machine and puts it behind the load balancer.

The result: no human is involved, and the outage is as short as it can be.

2

Who watches the watcher?

From the video▶ 0:58

The orchestrator is just another program on another machine. It can crash like anything else. If it does, nobody is watching the servers any more, and the next server failure stays broken.

The obvious answer is a second orchestrator that watches the first. But then who watches the second? A third. And who watches the third?

In the lesson’s words, this “goes out of hand quickly.” Adding one more watcher never solves the problem. It only moves it one step further away.

Key idea

We need a setup where, when one orchestrator goes down, another one takes over its job without anything outside the group telling it to. That setup is leader election.

3

The fix, part one: a leader and workers

From the video▶ 1:36

Instead of one orchestrator, run several machines that all run the same orchestrator code. Each machine is called a node. This arrangement is called a leader–follower setup: one node is the leader, and the others are workers (also called followers).

The two roles have different jobs.

Orchestrator workers

Watch the servers

Workers do the actual job from section 1. They ping the servers to check they are healthy. If a worker finds a dead server, it starts a new one and puts it behind the load balancer.

Orchestrator leader

Watches the workers

The leader keeps an eye on the workers. If a worker dies, the leader starts a new worker, which then picks up the job of watching servers.

So far two kinds of failure are covered. A dead server is handled by a worker. A dead worker is handled by the leader. One question is left: what if the leader dies?

4

The fix, part two: when the leader dies, elect a new one

From the video▶ 2:31

We do not add another watcher above the leader. Instead, the workers themselves notice that the leader is gone. They then run a leader election: a fixed procedure by which the group picks one of its own members to be the new leader.

One worker is promoted. It starts watching the other workers, and the system is whole again.

Now every kind of failure has an owner:

Who diesWho noticesWhat happens
A backend serverAn orchestrator workerThe worker starts a new server and puts it behind the load balancer
An orchestrator workerThe orchestrator leaderThe leader starts a new worker
The orchestrator leaderThe workersThe workers hold a leader election, and one of them becomes the new leader

How the workers choose is decided by the leader election algorithm you pick. There are many. The lesson names one, Bully, as the simplest (section 6.2 explains it).

“The base condition of your recursion” ▶ 3:53

The lesson uses a programming picture for this. A recursive function is one that calls itself. It must have a base condition — the case where it stops calling itself — or it runs for ever.

“Who watches X?” is that kind of question. Each answer (“Y does”) creates the same question again (“who watches Y?”). Leader election is the base condition: the point where the answer is no longer “someone else” but “the group watches itself.”

What you get

The system recovers from the failure of any single part with almost zero human help. That is what gives it very high availability — it stays up nearly all the time.

5

A general idea, not just for orchestrators

From the video▶ 4:52

The orchestrator is only the example. The same pattern fits any system that needs to recover by itself: a leader keeps an eye on the workers, the workers do the real work, and the workers replace the leader when it is gone.

The stand-up picture ▶ 5:16

The lesson compares it to a team at work. The manager normally runs the daily stand-up meeting. On a day the manager is absent, one of the team members steps up and runs it. So the stand-up never fails to happen.

If the manager is away for a month, that person keeps doing it for the month. When the manager returns, they can take the role back — or the stand-in simply carries on. Either way, there is always exactly one person running the meeting, and nobody from outside the team had to arrange it.

The lesson’s advice: read about leader election algorithms with this picture in mind, so you know why they exist.

6

Going deeper

Extra depth · not in the video

The lesson explains why leader election exists and leaves the “how” for you to read up on. These are the follow-up questions an interviewer is likely to ask next.

6.1 · How do the workers know the leader is dead?

A dead machine cannot announce that it is dead. So the leader does the opposite: it keeps announcing that it is alive. Every second or so it sends a tiny “I am here” message to the others. This message is called a heartbeat.

Each worker runs a timer. If no heartbeat arrives for some set time — the timeout — the worker assumes the leader is dead and starts an election.

Choosing the timeout is a real trade-off, because a worker cannot tell “dead” apart from “slow” or “the network dropped a few messages.” All it sees is silence.

Timeout too short

False alarms

One slow moment or a brief network hiccup looks like a death. The group holds elections it did not need, and leadership keeps jumping around.

Timeout too long

Slow recovery

The leader really is dead, but everyone waits a long time before acting. During that wait, nobody is doing the leader’s job.

A common shape is a heartbeat every second or so and a timeout of several missed heartbeats in a row.

6.2 · The Bully algorithm, step by step

This is the algorithm the lesson names. Every node gets a number (an ID). The rule is simple: the alive node with the biggest ID becomes the leader. It is called “bully” because a bigger node always pushes a smaller one aside.

Example: five nodes, 1 to 5. Node 5 is the leader, and it crashes. Node 2 happens to notice first.

If node 5 later comes back, it starts an election, finds nobody bigger than itself, and takes the leadership back. That is the “bully” part.

Good: very easy to understand and to build. Weak spot: it trusts silence. “No answer from a bigger node” is treated as “that node is dead”, which is not always true — see the next point.

6.3 · The real danger: two leaders at once

Imagine the leader is alive, but the network between it and some workers breaks. This is called a network partition: the machines are fine, but they are split into groups that cannot talk to each other.

The cut-off workers hear no heartbeat, so they elect a new leader. The old leader, on its side, still believes it is in charge. Now there are two leaders giving orders. This is called split brain. For an orchestrator it means doing things twice, like starting two replacement servers. For a database it is much worse: two nodes both accept writes, and the data splits apart.

The standard fix is one extra rule: a node may only become (or stay) leader if more than half of all the nodes agree. “More than half” is called a majority, or a quorum.

The cost: the small side does no leader work until the network heals. You are choosing “no leader for a while” over “two leaders”, because two leaders can do damage that is hard to undo.

This is also why such groups have an odd number of nodes, usually 3 or 5:

Nodes in the groupMajority neededFailures it survives
220 — losing either node stops elections
321
431 — no better than 3 nodes, but costs more
532

The plain Bully algorithm does not have this majority rule. The well-known algorithms used in production — Raft and Paxos — are built around it. In Raft, a node that suspects the leader is dead asks the others for their vote, and it becomes leader only when a majority votes for it.

6.4 · The old leader that does not know it was replaced

One more trap. The old leader may not be dead or cut off — it may just have frozen for ten seconds. When it wakes up, it still thinks it is the leader and carries on giving orders, while the new leader is doing the same.

The fix is to number the elections. Each time a new leader is chosen, a counter goes up by one. This counter is called a term (or epoch). The leader attaches its number to every order it sends. Anyone who receives an order with an older number than the latest one they have seen simply refuses it. When this number is checked by the thing being protected (a database, a storage system), it is called a fencing token.

6.5 · In practice: do not write your own

Getting leader election right in every edge case is hard, so real systems almost never hand-write it. They lean on a small, well-tested coordination service — a tiny, very reliable database whose whole purpose is to let other programs agree on things. The common ones are ZooKeeper, etcd and Consul. Inside, each of them runs a majority-based algorithm on 3 or 5 nodes of its own.

Your program then does something much simpler, usually called a lease:

  1. Every copy of your program tries to grab the same lock in the coordination service. Only one succeeds. That one is the leader.
  2. The lock expires after a few seconds unless its holder keeps renewing it. A lock with an expiry time is a lease.
  3. If the leader dies, it stops renewing. The lease runs out, another copy grabs it, and that copy is the new leader.

The renewals are the heartbeat, the expiry time is the timeout, and the coordination service supplies the majority rule. It is the same idea as the lesson, with the hard part borrowed.

6.6 · Where you have already met this

SystemWhat gets elected
Replicated databasesWhen the main database dies, one replica is promoted to be the new main. That is the “failover” from lesson 19, and choosing which replica is a leader election. MongoDB replica sets do this by majority vote.
KubernetesYou can run several copies of its scheduler and controller manager for safety. They use a lease so that only one copy is active at a time.
etcd, ZooKeeper, ConsulEach elects a leader among its own nodes. All writes go through that leader.

6.7 · Two limits worth saying out loud

  • There is a short gap. Between the leader dying and the new leader taking over, nobody does the leader’s job. In the lesson’s design this is mild: the workers keep watching the servers the whole time, and only “replace a dead worker” pauses for a few seconds.
  • “Zero human help” assumes someone survives. An election needs living nodes — with the majority rule, more than half of them. If every node sits in one data centre and that data centre is lost, nothing is left to hold an election. So spread the nodes across separate data centres.
7

Hands-on lab

Exercise from the video▶ 6:27 · code added here

The lesson sets a stretch exercise: implement a leader election yourself. It also gives a shortcut. You do not need several machines. Let each node be a thread (a separate line of work inside one program), make the leader thread kill itself, and watch the other threads elect a new leader.

Try it yourself first. Below is one small version in Python to compare against. It uses the Bully rule from section 6.2.

  1. Save this as election.py

    Five threads act as nodes 1 to 5. Node 5 starts as the leader. The leader keeps updating a shared “last heartbeat” time. The others watch that time.

    import threading, time

HEARTBEAT_EVERY = 0.5   # the leader says "I am alive" this often (seconds)
TIMEOUT = 1.5           # this long without a heartbeat = the leader is dead

lock = threading.Lock()
alive = set()           # ids of the nodes that are still running
leader = 5              # node 5 starts as the leader
last_heartbeat = time.time()

def node(my_id, crash_after=None):
    global leader, last_heartbeat
    started = time.time()
    with lock:
        alive.add(my_id)
    while True:
        time.sleep(HEARTBEAT_EVERY)
        with lock:
            if crash_after and time.time() - started > crash_after:
                alive.discard(my_id)
                print(f"node {my_id}: crashed")
                return
            if leader == my_id:
                last_heartbeat = time.time()             # leader: send a heartbeat
            elif time.time() - last_heartbeat > TIMEOUT:
                # follower: the leader is silent, so start an election.
                # Bully rule: ask every node with a bigger id. If none is alive, I win.
                bigger = [n for n in alive if n > my_id]
                if not bigger:
                    leader = my_id
                    last_heartbeat = time.time()
                    print(f"node {my_id}: leader is silent, nobody bigger answered -> I am the leader")

print("node 5: I am the leader")
crash_times = {5: 3, 4: 7}                                # node 5 dies at 3 s, node 4 at 7 s
for i in range(1, 6):
    threading.Thread(target=node, args=(i, crash_times.get(i)), daemon=True).start()
time.sleep(11)
print(f"still alive: {sorted(alive)}, leader: node {leader}")
  2. Run it

    It runs for 11 seconds. Nobody tells the others that the leader died; they work it out from the missing heartbeat, and the group repairs itself twice.

    python3 election.py
    node 5: I am the leader
node 5: crashed
node 4: leader is silent, nobody bigger answered -> I am the leader
node 4: crashed
node 3: leader is silent, nobody bigger answered -> I am the leader
still alive: [1, 2, 3], leader: node 3
  3. Notice what is faked

    The threads share memory, so “ask the bigger nodes” is just a look at the alive set, and it is always correct. Real machines only have messages that can be slow or lost. That gap is exactly where sections 6.3 and 6.4 come from.

Try next

Change TIMEOUT to 0.4 (shorter than the heartbeat) and watch followers lose patience with a healthy leader. Bring node 5 back after 9 seconds and make it take the leadership back. Then add the majority rule: a node may only become leader if more than half of the five nodes are alive.

8

In the interview

Extra depth · not in the video
What to say when you draw anything that “manages” other things

“This component watches the others, so if it dies nothing gets repaired. I’d run three copies of it and let them elect a leader using a lease in etcd or ZooKeeper. Only the leader acts. If it dies, its lease expires in a few seconds and another copy takes over, with no human involved. Because the lease is backed by a majority vote, a network split cannot give us two leaders.”

Likely follow-ups. Answer each out loud before opening it.

Why not just add a second orchestrator to watch the first?

Because the second one can die too, and then you need a third to watch it. Every watcher you add needs its own watcher.

Leader election ends the chain: the copies watch each other and replace their own leader, so nothing outside the group is needed.

Why have one leader at all? Why not let every copy act?

If three copies all see the same dead server and all react, you get three replacement servers. Some jobs need exactly one decision-maker.

A single leader is the simplest way to get that. The other copies are there only so that a leader always exists.

How does a node know the leader is dead?

It cannot know for sure. The leader sends a heartbeat every second or so, and a node that hears nothing for a set timeout assumes it is dead.

Silence can also mean a slow leader or a broken network. A short timeout gives false alarms; a long one gives slow recovery.

What is split brain, and how do you prevent it?

Two nodes both believe they are the leader, usually after a network split, and both give orders.

Prevent it with a majority rule: nobody is leader without more than half of the nodes behind them, and two groups cannot both have more than half. Add an election number to every order so that a stale leader’s orders are refused.

Why three or five nodes? Why not two or four?

With a majority rule, two nodes survive zero failures: lose one and the other is not “more than half.” Three survive one failure.

Four also survive only one, so the fourth node adds cost without adding safety. Five survive two.

Is the system down while the election is running?

Only the leader’s own job pauses, and only for a few seconds: the timeout plus the election itself.

What that means depends on the job. For an orchestrator, repairs are briefly delayed but user traffic is untouched. For a database, writes are refused until the new main is chosen.

Explain the Bully algorithm in three sentences.

Every node has an ID, and the alive node with the biggest ID should be leader. A node that notices the leader is gone sends “election” to all bigger nodes; if any of them answers, it backs off and they repeat the same step.

A node that gets no answer from anyone bigger declares itself leader and tells everyone.
